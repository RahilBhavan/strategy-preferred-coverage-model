Chapter 1

The finance you need first

Everything about Strategy rests on about ten ideas. Learn these first and every later chapter becomes a combination of them.

No prior finance knowledge is assumed. Each idea gets a plain definition, a small example with made-up numbers, and the reason it matters for Strategy.

1. A company is a pile of assets with claims on it

A company owns things (cash, bitcoin, software) and owes things. Everyone who gave it money holds a claim. The rule that matters most is simple: assets = claims. If the assets shrink, somebody's claim shrinks. Finance is mostly about deciding whose.

2. Debt, preferred and common are three deals

3. Seniority decides who gets paid in a bad outcome

Senior claims are paid in full before junior ones get anything. This order is written into legal documents (bond indentures for debt, certificates of designation for preferred stock). A junior claim is riskier, so buyers demand a higher return for it. That's why Strategy's most junior preferred (STRD) pays more than a more senior one (STRK).

4. Price and yield move in opposite directions

A preferred with a $100 face value (also called stated amount or liquidation preference) and a 10% rate pays $10 a year no matter what it trades at. What changes is the yield a buyer earns: the $10 divided by the price they paid.

5. Leverage cuts both ways

Leverage means using borrowed or fixed-cost money to own more of something. If the asset earns more than the money costs, owners gain more than the asset did. If it earns less, owners lose more. Fixed claims don't shrink when the asset does. Chapter 7 works this through with Strategy's numbers.

6. Every source of money has a cost

The cost of capital is what a company gives up to get money. For debt it's the interest rate. For preferred it's the dividend rate. For common stock it's harder to see: there's no payment, but new shares give away a slice of all future value. A company creates value only if what it does with the money earns more than that cost. For Strategy the "investment" is bitcoin, so the test is whether bitcoin rises faster than its cost of capital.

7. A convertible is a bond with an option attached

8. "Perpetual" means never paying the money back

All five Strategy preferreds are perpetual. There's no maturity date, so Strategy never has to find $14B to repay them. The price is paying the dividend forever, unless Strategy chooses to buy them back. That trade removes refinancing risk, the danger of a big repayment falling due in a bad market. That's a big economic advantage of preferreds over bonds, even though Strategy hasn't named it as a reason.

9. NAV and premium

Net asset value (NAV) is what the assets would be worth to owners if you sold them all and paid every claim. For Strategy that's roughly bitcoin plus cash minus debt and preferred. The premium is how much more than that the market pays for the shares. mNAV is the ratio: 1.0x means no premium, 1.5x means the market pays $1.50 for each $1 of net assets. A premium lets a company sell shares for more than the assets behind them (Chapter 6).

10. How public companies actually raise money

A company files a shelf registration with the SEC that lets it sell securities over time. It can then do a large one-off offering (an IPO for a new preferred, for example) or an at-the-market (ATM) program, which sells small amounts into normal trading every day through a broker. Strategy uses ATMs heavily because they are cheap, quick and quiet. The limit is that each day's sales are a small share of trading volume, and buyers have to want the shares.

Check yourself

Assets fall 20%. Who loses money first: debt, preferred or common?

Common. Its claim is whatever's left, so it absorbs losses until it's gone. Only then do preferred, and then debt, lose anything.

A preferred pays $8 a year and trades at $64. What's its yield, and what does the price suggest?

12.5%. It trades well below its $100 face value, so the market sees it as risky (or rates are high).

Why could Strategy borrow at 0% interest?

Buyers got a conversion option on a very volatile stock. The option was worth more to them than a coupon.
