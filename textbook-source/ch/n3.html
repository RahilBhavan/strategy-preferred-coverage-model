Chapter 14

2024–2025: spending the premium

When the market pays three times net assets for your stock, you sell stock. When that window might close, you build other ways to raise money. That's this era in two sentences.

Selling stock at about 3.4x

The economic catch. Every sale at a premium transfers value from new buyers to old holders. New buyers do it willingly only if they expect the premium to persist. So the strategy works as long as the market believes it will keep working.

$3B of 0% convertibles due 2029

The trade. In Nov 2024 Strategy borrowed $3B at 0% interest, convertible at about $672 a share, roughly 55% above MSTR's price at the time.

What it saved. Interest. At even 6%, $3B costs $180M a year.

How it turned out. MSTR fell to about $151 by October 2026, far below $672. The option is nearly worthless, so the notes behave like 0% debt. Holders can put them in June 2028. Strategy repurchased $1.5B of them in May 2026, leaving $1.5B. In effect, the 2024 decision traded a $180M-a-year interest bill for a large principal question in 2028.

Joining the Nasdaq-100

The economics. Index funds tracking the Nasdaq-100 must hold MSTR. That creates steady passive demand, which supports both the price and the trading volume that ATM sales depend on. The flip side showed up in Jan 2026, when MSCI froze its index share counts for bitcoin-heavy companies. Index decisions directly affect how much stock Strategy can sell.

Fair-value accounting and the minimum tax

The risk. The 15% corporate alternative minimum tax is based on book profit. Under fair-value accounting, every $10,000 rise in bitcoin is about $8.5B of paper profit for Strategy. Taxed at 15%, that could have meant roughly $1.3B of cash tax on gains it never sold. That's an illustration, not a calculated liability.

The outcome. After IRS interim guidance (Notice 2025-49), Strategy said in Oct 2025 it no longer expects to owe the tax on unrealized gains. That removed a potential cash drain the size of a year's dividends.

Launching preferreds below face value

The economics. As Chapter 12 shows, the five launches created about $0.9B more claim than cash raised. Why accept that? Because a brand-new security from a B- rated bitcoin company needed a higher starting yield to win buyers. Selling at a discount raises the buyer's yield without printing a higher rate, which keeps the stated rate (and the long-run cost on any later sales at $100) lower.

Making STRC variable

The economics. A fixed-rate preferred's price drifts with interest rates and credit worries. Once it trades below $100, every new sale creates more claim than cash, so issuance stalls. A variable rate lets management push the price back to $100 and keep selling every day. STRC has $17.5B of unsold ATM capacity for that reason.

The cost. The rate rose from 9% to 12% in a year: about $264M a year more at today's size. That is the price of a security that can always be sold.

Selling in euros

The economics. A new investor pool means more demand and potentially a lower rate than selling a sixth US preferred into the same market. The cost is currency risk. So far it has worked in Strategy's favour: the euro weakened after launch, shrinking STRE's dollar claim by about $22M.
